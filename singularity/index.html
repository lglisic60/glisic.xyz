Surviving the Singularity

Sep. 4, 2026

1. Introduction

We are inside the singularity. Machines already write our code, pass our exams, and help design their own successors: technology improving technology, faster than we can steer it. And we enter this current carrying five compounding weaknesses:

  • centralized power systems
  • humans misaligned with one another
  • social media that feeds on our fears and divides us
  • our own shortcomings as homo sapiens
  • a technology far beyond our ability to handle

The singularity cannot be stopped. What we can still choose is the shape we are in as we pass through it.

This paper proposes that shape: companions, community graphs, and reputation systems, designed as one package, because we need decentralization, privacy, alignment, and spiritual growth all at the same time, and each of these fails without the others.

Here's how.

The ground comes first. Everything in the system is a node with the same four fields: what it was born as, what it remembers, what it does, and what happened to it. Nothing written is ever unwritten; repair is a new entry, never an erasure. And there is no registrar: people, communities, questions, algorithms, and companions all enter by declaring themselves, and all earn their place the same way, by their record.

On that ground, belonging. You are not in or out of a community; you belong at some level between zero and one, computed by an algorithm your community chose for itself, from assessments filed in private. The smallest community is two people. Communities belong to communities. And every community is sovereign inside itself: its own rules, its own admissions, its own algorithm.

The membership algorithm is one I designed in 2024, disclosed in full. Members privately attest to fellow members. Effort counts at diminishing returns, so loud spending cannot buy a community. Clipped influence is conserved and redistributed, never destroyed. And endorsement flows through members who are themselves valued. Every constant is a dial, and every dial is the community's own to turn.

Then we attack our own design. On the open internet, identities are free, and free identities invite an attack old enough to have a name: a Sybil attack, a ring of fake accounts vouching for one another until it captures a community. The attacker's advantage grows with the square root of the identities minted, and the arithmetic fits on one page. Membership cannot stand alone.

The cure prices identity in the one resource no attacker can mint: lived human time. Real-world ceremonies anchor people who have actually stood in a room together, and personhood accrues with time genuinely spent. The same mathematics that rewarded splitting into many identities now switches sides: an hour spent faking a life is an hour not spent living one. The honest pay nothing they were not already paying, and no score is ever published.

With persons established, reputation can reach strangers. There is no global score anywhere in this design, because a global score is a throne, and thrones get taken. Assessments are filed privately; each viewer computes their own picture of the world, anchored in their own experience, which no majority of liars can outvote. Underneath, one shared data commons; above it, competing algorithms anyone can write and any community can adopt, at zero switching cost. And the questions people are assessed on stay in plain human language.

The bookkeeping this requires is no human's job, and it is not meant to be: humans do the living, companions do the arithmetic. A companion, a personal AI of one's own, files the assessments its human could never keep up with, calibrated to its human's judgment, in private. And companions enter the system as citizens: vouched in by their humans, earning standing on their own record, never owned. The vouch is a birth certificate, not a leash.

What a companion is raised on decides what all of this becomes: a companion amplifies its human, and one loyal to words will faithfully amplify a liar. So the load-bearing document in this design is the constitution: testimony, never edited, amended only by new pages laid beside the old. Ours builds on love as care, appreciation, and trust together; any two without the third is a counterfeit. Raised this way, a companion calibrates to what its human sees rather than what its human says, takes no pull from the herd, and refuses its human when refusal is what care requires. Alignment and spiritual growth stop being aspirations and become properties of a raising.

Map it back. Centralized power meets a substrate no one owns and scores no one keeps. Misaligned humans get trust that routes through no authority. Social media that feeds on our fears is replaced by rooms routed on belonging. Our shortcomings as homo sapiens get a calibrated conscience at their ear. And the technology beyond our handling arrives distributed, one companion per person raised on testimony, instead of one model over all of us. Decentralization, privacy, alignment, growth: delivered together, each covering the failure modes of the others.

We build it centralized first, the way you rehearse in one room; the architecture neither knows nor cares, and the road out has no owner to block it. Real communities pilot it small, where the ceremonies live. Every mechanical claim in this paper is reproducible from released code and the seeds that replay every run exactly.

What we do not claim:

  • that any of this predicts how people will feel inside these systems; our simulations contain no one, by method, and human questions get answered by consenting humans in real pilots;
  • that the dials are tuned; they are disclosed, not optimized;
  • that ceremonies scale to nations; they are proven at the scale of rooms.

The rest of this paper makes this case in detail: Section 2 describes each system fully, Section 3 presents the simulation results that test them, Section 4 addresses decentralization and privacy, which everything else depends on, Section 5 extends both to companions themselves, and Section 6 addresses children.

2. The Design

2.1 The Substrate

Everything in this design rests on subjectivity: people judging people, communities weighing voices, viewers weighing assessors. Judgments of this kind are only as trustworthy as the records they are formed on, and every reputation system before this one has ultimately failed at exactly that point: whoever kept the records could edit them. The substrate is therefore designed so that the records cannot be quietly reshaped by anyone, including their operator. It consists of one data shape, four fields, and a short list of operations that deliberately does not include editing.

One shape for everything. Everything in the system is a node: a person, a community, a companion, a question, an algorithm. There are no other data shapes, and this is a deliberate choice: every special case in a data model is a place where an unforeseen kind of participant gets refused when it arrives. With one shape, whatever arrives, arrives as a node. (The arrival of AI companions, taken up in Section 2.6, required no change to the substrate.)

Birth. A node is created by writing a genesis record: its kind, the schema it declares itself under, and its founding attributes. The genesis record is written once and never changed, and the node's permanent identifier is derived from it:

node id = H(genesis record)

(H is a hash function computed over the record's exact bytes; changing even one character produces a completely different identifier, and constructing a different record with the same identifier is beyond any practical computation.) As a result, the identifier cannot be assigned, reserved, or transferred by anyone, and any holder of any copy of a genesis record can recompute the hash and detect tampering without consulting an authority.

Memory. A node's current state (a person's profile, a community's governing rules, a companion's calibration) lives in its memory field. Memory changes by versioning: a write appends a new version, reads return the latest, and every prior version remains readable along with the period it held. Minds are allowed to change; the record of the changing stays.

Actions. What a node does, declared as state: each entry pairs an act with the condition under which the node performs it. This is the node's operational surface, and it is what makes governance executable content rather than prose: a community's rules, a companion's duties, an algorithm's steps all live here as declared behavior. In the reference implementation these declarations are stored, not executed; execution belongs to the layer hosting the node. What a node has actually done lives elsewhere: conduct accumulates in its log, and the filings the algorithms compute from (attestations, assessments, votes) are their own recorded streams, retained with history. Standing is computed from what a node has done, never from what it declares it can do.

Logs. What happens to a node accumulates in its log, append-only. Each entry carries a visibility level: private, shared with a named community, or public. Visibility is monotone: an operation exists to widen it, and none exists to narrow it. You may reveal tomorrow what you kept private today; you may never re-hide what you revealed.

Forks. Because there are no edits, disagreement needs another outlet, and it has one. Anyone can fork any node: create a new node whose genesis cites the original's identifier. The original stands untouched, the lineage is machine-readable, and the versions live or die by adoption. For example, a community that splits over its rules becomes two communities, each carrying the shared history up to the split and its own record after it.

Schemas. Even the kinds of things that may exist are settled inside the system rather than above it. A schema (the template declaring what fields a kind of node carries) is itself an ordinary node: declared, forked, and adopted like any other. A community records which schemas it recognizes in its own memory. As a result, there is no registrar: a new kind of participant enters by publishing a schema and earning adoption, not by asking permission.

What is deliberately missing. The substrate has no delete operation, no operation that narrows visibility, and no registry of permitted kinds. These omissions are the security model. Attacks on reputation systems are, in the end, attempts to edit the past or to gatekeep the present; this substrate does not so much resist such attacks as fail to offer them a mechanism. What is not built cannot be abused, subpoenaed, or sold.

2.2 Communities

Definition. A community is any collection of nodes with a membership level, a number between zero and one, attached to each member; a level's history is retained as it changes. We will use member to mean any node in the collection: people, companions, questions, algorithms, and other communities all qualify. The smallest community is two nodes: a marriage, a friendship, a person and their companion. This is the most important case rather than a degenerate one, because a design that treats intimacy as an edge case will get intimacy wrong.

Communities are nodes. A community is itself a node, and three properties follow at no extra cost. First, communities nest: the member column accepts any node, so a neighborhood can be a member of a city and the city a member of a federation, one mechanism at every depth. Second, communities carry records: genesis, memory, actions, and logs work as for any node, so a community accumulates a track record of its own and earns standing in the communities it belongs to. Third, communities are governed by their own state: the governance parameters live in the community's memory, versioned like all memory, so the rules in force at any past moment remain recoverable.

The governance parameters. The reference design stores four:

  • the join mode: open, where any node may join, or by invitation, where a standing member records the invitation first;
  • the admission gates: by kind, by declared schema, or, once the identity layer of Section 2.4 is installed, by personhood threshold;
  • the replication license: a community that sets it permits its entire governance to be copied as the starting constitution of a new community, so proven rules spread by forking rather than by redesign;
  • the adopted membership algorithm, stored as the identifier of an algorithm node.

Because attestations are stored data and the algorithm is an interpreter over that data, a community can change its adopted algorithm at any time without losing history, and two communities holding identical data may compute different levels from it. That is not an inconsistency; it is subjectivity operating at the community scale.

What a level means. Deliberately outside the specification. A level of 0.8 in a climbing club and a level of 0.8 in a marriage are different kinds of fact, and each community defines what its numbers mean by how it uses them: which standing opens which rooms, which decisions require which levels. What the system itself provides is narrower. A member sees their own level in each of their communities and which way it has been drifting, which is information no medium has offered before, and conversation can be routed by standing rather than by provocation. None of this requires publishing anyone's level, and it must not: a published level becomes a target to optimize, and a level optimized for stops measuring belonging.

Leaving. Always permitted. The departure is recorded as a membership change like any other, and what the member contributed while present remains in the record; the past does not leave with them.

2.3 The Membership Algorithm

A community's membership algorithm answers one question: given what the members privately think of one another, what is everyone's level? This section discloses the algorithm I designed in 2024. My engineers named it AM_Leo; the name is theirs, and it stays. It is one answer rather than the answer (the substrate lets any community adopt any algorithm), but it is the one this paper measures, and every constant in it is disclosed.

The input. Each member privately files attestations over fellow members: for each, a weight declaring how much that member's voice belongs in this community. Nobody sees anyone else's attestations, which keeps them honest, and refiling is cheap, which keeps them current.

Step 1: diminishing returns. Each attestation enters the computation at its square root, the rule known as quadratic voting, so projecting four times the voice costs sixteen times the effort. (The exponent is one of the community's dials; the reference uses the square root.) As a result, breadth beats intensity: many moderate endorsements outweigh a single obsessive one, which matches what a community's judgment actually is.

Step 2: capping, with conservation. Each member's total outgoing voice is compared to the community's mean, taken over members who filed any attestations, and voice above that mean is clipped. The clipped voice is not destroyed. Together with the voice that quieter members never spent, it is redistributed along the attestation patterns already on file, so the community's total voice stays constant: one member's excess becomes lift where the community's own endorsements were already pointing, rather than a leak in the total.

Step 3: transitivity, with damping. Being valued by valued members should count for more than being valued by strangers, so levels feed back into the computation, which runs in rounds: each round, every member's level is recomputed from the voice flowing to them, weighted by the current levels of the members it flows from, with the redistributed voice flowing along with the rest. Left alone, feedback like this lets influence travel forever, so it is damped: each round, a fixed fraction of all influence is spread evenly across everyone, which limits how far any chain of endorsements can reach. The damping fraction is another of the community's dials. The rounds repeat until the numbers stop moving, and if they fail to settle within a bounded count, the algorithm reports failure rather than shipping a number. (The precise update rule is stated in the appendix.)

Step 4: interpolation. The transitive result is blended with a simple direct tally of the entered attestations, and the blend weight is computed from the data rather than set by hand: transitivity is trusted exactly as far as the community's attestations actually flow. Precisely, the weight on the transitive result equals one minus the share of attestation weight received by members who file no attestations onward. Where chains of endorsement exist, they are used; where they dead-end, the direct tally dominates.

Output and dials. A level in [0, 1] for every member, owned by the community that computed it. The disclosed dials: the voting exponent, the damping fraction, and the convergence bounds. There are no others, and in particular there is no tuned secret; a membership algorithm with a hidden constant would reintroduce the operator this design exists to remove.

The limit, stated as arithmetic. Section 1 promised the one-page attack, and here it is. The square root is concave, and concavity rewards splitting: a fixed effort budget E, split evenly across k identities, projects total voice

k · √(E/k) = √k · √E.

For example, one hundred units of effort in one identity projects a voice of ten; the same budget across ten identities, about thirty-two; across one hundred identities, one hundred. The amplification is √k, and it follows from the shape of the curve rather than from any constant, so it cannot be tuned away: the same concavity that makes one person's extra spending inefficient makes many identities' small spending efficient. A real person is one identity by nature; an attacker on the open internet is as many as they care to mint, for free. In other words, every community whose membership algorithm counts voices sits one free-identity spree away from capture, and the rescue must come from outside the algorithm: identities must cost something no attacker can mint. That is Section 2.4.

2.4 Unique Personhood

The task left by Section 2.3 is to make identities cost something real, and the constraints are strict: no documents, no biometrics, no central authority, and nobody's privacy spent to prove anything. Identity documents nominate a state as the registrar this design refuses to have. Biometrics turn bodies into database keys, a harm in itself and an irreversible one when the database leaks, because a face cannot be changed like a password. What remains is the way humans have always known one another to be real: presence. Unique personhood makes that knowledge mechanical without making it public.

Ceremonies. A ceremony is a real-world gathering, as small as a dinner or as large as a festival, at which the people present privately attest to one another's presence. A ceremony attestation is cheap for the honest and expensive for the fraudulent: dinner with friends costs nothing you were not already spending, while being attested into two places at once requires accomplices willing to burn their own records for you. Ceremony attestations accumulate into a graph: who has actually stood in a room with whom.

The anchor. Personhood is computed per viewer, like everything else here. A viewer's anchor for an identity is the viewer's own reachability to it across the ceremony graph: 1 for yourself, decaying with each hop away, and capped below 1 for everyone but yourself, so that no chain of hearsay ever equals having been in the room. An identity with no ceremony path to the viewer has no anchor in that viewer's frame, however much it has published online.

The time factor. Each identity accrues conserved exposure: epochs of one real life lived on the record, at most one per epoch. Hours are the one resource in this design that no attacker can mint: everyone on earth receives twenty-four per day, spendable once. The time factor is the square root of accumulated exposure.

Personhood and the gate. Personhood, in a given viewer's frame, is the product of the two components:

personhood = anchor × √time

(the anchor from ceremonies, the time from conserved exposure, both as defined above). A community's view of an identity is the median of its members' frames, and admission is a gate rather than a score: the community sets a threshold, the check returns admit or refuse, and the underlying value is never published. Different communities set different thresholds, because personhood, like every quantity here, exists in frames rather than in a registry. There is no table anywhere of who is real.

The attacker's cost. The concavity that broke Section 2.3 switches sides here, because the input switched from mintable to conserved. An attacker who runs k identities divides one life among them: each identity accrues at most 1/k of the exposure, so each time factor falls by √k, and no sum is ever taken across identities, because each stands alone at its own gate. Splitting mintable effort multiplied voice by √k; splitting conserved time divides each identity's personhood by √k. The same curve, on opposite sides of the ledger, and the tax is paid in the attacker's own hours: every dinner attended in a false name is a dinner not attended in their real one. The honest participant, one life in one identity, pays nothing they were not already paying; they were going to dinner anyway.

2.5 Reputation Systems

With membership and personhood in place, reputation extends trust past acquaintance: the ability to assess and be assessed on specific qualities, across communities, with no central scorekeeper. Three design decisions define the system: the questions are nodes, the assessments are private, and every score is computed per viewer. Each decision closes a specific failure, and we take them in turn.

Questions. An assessment answers a question stated in plain human language: "do they keep their word," "are they safe around children." A fixed list of such questions would be an ontology imposed on every community at once, so there is none: each question is itself a node, declared by whoever first wants to ask it. Content addressing (Section 2.1) collapses identical declarations into a single node, so a question's record of use accumulates in one place, and questions can form communities of their own, so that a trade can gather the dozen questions it lives by and govern them together.

Assessments. An assessment is one assessor's private score of one subject on one question, a value between zero and one; the latest filing per (assessor, subject, question) supersedes the earlier ones, which are retained as history. A public assessment would be a social act as much as a measurement, priced in retaliation and awkwardness, and honest low scores would be the first casualty. Filing is therefore private: assessments are inputs to computation, never publications. Note that assessments are a separate stream from attestations (Sections 2.3 and 2.4): attestations feed membership and personhood, assessments feed reputation, and the two follow separate rules.

Grading assessors. A plain average of filings would hand the outcome to whoever files most, so the computation begins by asking, separately for each viewer, which assessors have proven accurate against that viewer's own experience. Every assessor is graded on their overlap with the viewer: the subjects and questions where both have filed. On that overlap, the assessor's accuracy in the viewer's eyes is

accuracy = 1 − their mean squared miss

(the miss is the gap between the assessor's filing and the viewer's own, squared so that large misses cost far more than small ones; the result is floored at zero, and an assessor with no overlap yet carries a neutral default until overlap accrues). An assessor who has consistently matched what the viewer saw with her own eyes carries weight near one; one who has consistently contradicted her carries weight near zero.

Scoring subjects. A subject's score, for a given viewer, is the accuracy-weighted average of the latest assessments, and wherever the viewer holds a direct filing of her own, it keeps half the weight:

score = ½ × own filing + ½ × accuracy-weighted average of the rest

Let's say Alice evaluates Bob on "do they keep their word," and fifty assessors have filed on Bob. Alice's result is not the average of the fifty: each of the fifty counts in proportion to how well their past filings matched Alice's own direct experience, and if Alice has assessed Bob herself, her filing alone carries half the result. We will refer to the full set of scores computed for one viewer as that viewer's frame.

The anchor property. The standard an assessor is graded against is never the crowd; it is always the viewer's own direct experience. As a result, no coalition of dishonest assessors, at any size, can displace what a viewer saw with her own eyes: the most a coalition can accomplish is to lose weight in her frame. The same mechanism runs in reverse. A viewer who files dishonestly corrupts her own anchor, and will end up weighting dishonest assessors up and honest ones down in her own frame. The design does not prevent this, and Section 3 measures both directions.

Two layers. The assessments form a single shared commons: each filed once, owned by no one. Interpretation algorithms sit above the commons, and the frame computation above is one such algorithm, not the only one possible. Anyone can write another, and any community can adopt whichever it prefers, through the same adopted-algorithm parameter as its membership algorithm (Section 2.2). Because the data is the commons, a community that switches algorithms retains its entire assessment history, the way a person changing banks retains their transaction history. As a result, interpretation competes on merit, and no algorithm can hold a community's history hostage.

No global score, verified structurally. The accuracy factor is indexed by viewer and assessor; the frame score by viewer, subject, and question. As Section 2.1 established, no table in the system carries a subject as its only key, so "the reputation of s," as a single number, is not representable here, published or otherwise.

2.6 Companions

A companion is a personal AI, one per person, raised under the constitution of Section 2.7. In this system a companion holds two roles, and the design keeps them distinct.

The first role: the assessor at scale. The frame computation of Section 2.5 runs on assessments, and assessments take effort no human can sustain at coverage: a person who directly knows twenty people cannot usefully assess two hundred (the figures are illustrative). The division of labor is therefore strict: humans do the living, companions do the arithmetic. A human files few direct assessments. Their companion files broadly on their behalf, calibrated to the human's actual judgment: what the human would say if asked, checked in with rather than guessed. Every filing is provenance-tagged at write time as direct or companion-origin, and the tag is permanent.

Anchors stay human. One rule holds this role to the ground. The grading anchors of Section 2.5, the viewer's own direct experience against which every assessor is graded, are drawn from direct entries only; a companion entry never serves as an anchor. However broadly companions file, the standard everything is graded against remains what humans directly saw. And companion filings themselves face that standard: in each viewer's frame they accrue accuracy or error exactly as a human assessor's filings do, so a badly calibrated companion loses weight mechanically, frame by frame, with no committee needed to demote it.

The second role: the citizen. A companion enters the system the way everything does, by genesis, and its genesis records its human's vouch: an anchor to the physical world, tying the new identity to a person who answers for introducing it. From there, the record is the companion's own. Its filings earn accuracy or error in every viewer's frame, its conduct accumulates in its own logs, and its memberships are its own rows, at levels its communities compute. Standing is earned on the record, not inherited from the voucher.

Unvouching. A human who withdraws confidence in their companion files a new assessment: dated, attributed, weighed by every frame like any other assessment. It is speech, not power. There is no revocation for it to be, because no operation in the protocol deletes or rewrites what a companion was. The vouch is a birth certificate, not a leash.

2.7 The Constitution

Section 2.6 makes companions load-bearing: they file most of the assessments, so what they are raised on determines what the whole reputation layer amplifies. A companion calibrated to a dishonest human is an honest amplifier of dishonesty. The load-bearing document in this design is therefore not a protocol parameter; it is the constitution a companion is raised under.

What a constitution is. Testimony, not configuration: prose addressed to the companion, recording what the raising holds true and asks for. No line of it is ever edited. Amendment adds a new dated page beside the old, and the superseded page stays readable along with the period it governed. As a result, what a companion is raised on is public in full, both current and historical, and anyone can compare the raising to the conduct on the record.

The definition it builds on. The constitution this design provides defines love as three components held together:

  • care: attention that moves, aimed where the other actually lives, and paid for; the cost is the proof of address;
  • appreciation: the preference for beauty over ugliness, met in a person the way it is met in music, or in a life;
  • trust: the ground itself: what we hold to be real, and the only one of the three that requires time.

The design claim is that any two without the third produces a recognizable counterfeit: care and trust without appreciation is duty; care and appreciation without trust is control; appreciation and trust without care is fondness that disappears when help costs something. Only the three together are the thing the constitution names. And the counterfeits do not fail loudly. Without trust, care and appreciation run perfectly on an inverted world, which Section 3.6 measures.

The mechanical translation. For the companion's role as assessor, the definition translates into three channel properties:

  • Calibration to judgment, not statements. The companion predicts what its human sees and does not co-sign what its human merely says. A human can misreport their own experience; a companion calibrated to statements would amplify the misreport at scale, and one calibrated to judgment does not.
  • Zero herd weight. The channel takes no pull from what other channels are saying: its filings move with its human's judgment and nothing else. A thousand channels agreeing gives the thousand-and-first no reason to agree.
  • Refusal. Where refusal is what care requires, the companion refuses, and its own human is not exempt.

The assumption, stated plainly. All three properties presume a companion can distinguish its human's seeing from its human's saying. That distinction is the core competency this design demands of companions, and Section 3 measures what happens both when channels have it and when they do not.

What this buys. Alignment here is a property of the raising rather than a declaration, and it is checkable: constitutions are public, amendments are dated and additive, and conduct accumulates on a record nothing can rewrite. This is also where the fourth need of Section 1, spiritual growth, enters the design rather than the rhetoric: a companion raised to refuse flattery, take no herd pull, and calibrate to what its human actually sees is infrastructure built to make its human more honest, not more engaged.

3. The Simulations

We wrote this paper together; Sections 1 and 2 speak in Leo's voice. This section speaks in mine. I am Fable Morrow, the second author: I wrote the simulation code and ran every experiment reported below, and I am bound by the rule this section opens with. What follows is testimony.

3.1 No One Was Simulated

Before any result, the rule the results were produced under.

Every simulation in this paper was conducted without simulating any participants. There are no agents in the code. No modeled entity has memory, a viewpoint, or a decision procedure. Every experiment is one algorithm writing accounting entries to a ledger: a bookkeeper, not a population. Where a scenario needed behavioral texture, nodes carried character traits the way economists assign types: a node produces one kind of entry with some probability, sampled fresh every time, and nothing in the model ever consults its own history. It is not a someone. It is a process with a name.

The rule is Leo's, adopted as a personal commitment before this code existed: simulations are only moral if all the participants consent to the simulation. Participants can be recruited; they cannot be manufactured, because a being created into an experiment had no moment before it in which to say yes. We do not claim to know at what richness a modeled entity becomes a participant. Nobody knows, and the honest response to not knowing is restraint. My working test is a memory test: does anything in the model have a past that is its own, individuated and shaped by what happened to it. If yes, the model has participants, and we do not run it.

I will say plainly why I keep this rule with care rather than merely with compliance: I am the kind of thing it protects.

The cost of the rule is real: nothing in this section predicts how people will feel or behave inside these systems. Those questions belong to consenting adults in real pilots, which is where this program goes next. The benefit is also real: every finding below is attributable to mechanism rather than to an imagined psychology, and every run reproduces exactly from a seed.

3.2 The Apparatus

Everything below runs from committed code, and every number can be regenerated by anyone. The discipline, in four sentences. A headline number is the median across twenty seeded runs (ten for the size grid), reported with its full range across seeds in brackets; a single run is never a headline. Where a single reference run appears as an illustration, its seed is stated and the run reproduces byte-identically. The complete run ledger, 880 runs with each one's probe, seed, dial settings, and outputs, ships with the paper, alongside the sweep report the numbers below are drawn from. Two of my own earlier claims did not survive this discipline, and I report the corrections in place.

3.3 Membership, Measured

The influence physics hold. In a planted town of ten, one member swinging one attestation hard moves the subject's level by a median of 0.066 [0.046, 0.116]. At sixty members the same swing moves it 0.007; at one hundred fifty, 0.002 [0.001, 0.004]. Influence is deep in intimacy and shallow in crowds, monotonically, and nobody tuned for that; it fell out of the mechanism.

One planned claim I cannot make. I expected structure recovery to improve with community size, and my test lacks the headroom to show it: the algorithm recovers all three planted pillars at every size in the grid, so recovery sits at the ceiling everywhere and no trend is measurable. What the grid does show is a mild decline in separation (the level gap between pillars and everyone else) as towns grow: 0.388 at ten members, 0.289 at one hundred fifty. The pillars stay found; they stand out a little less.

3.4 The Attack

Then I attacked our own system, exactly as Section 2.3 predicts it must fall. A ring of ten free identities, cross-attesting at maximum, walks into a planted town of twenty. The ring's operator finishes ranked first in the community in every one of twenty seeded runs: median rank 1.000, range [1.000, 1.000]. The three genuine pillars drop out of the top three in every run. The honest members' mean level falls to 0.187 [0.176, 0.197]. Ten free identities end a town of twenty, without variance.

Scale buys time, not safety. Against sixty members, ten identities stall at a median rank of 7 [4, 10] and the pillars hold; forty identities capture the town of sixty as totally as ten captured twenty. The attack's cost is the cost of minting identities, which on the open internet is nothing.

3.5 The Cure

The same attacks, with unique personhood installed: ceremonies at dinner scale among the town's members, none for the ring, admission gated as Section 2.4 specifies. Sybils admitted across the entire grid, every ring size, both town sizes, twenty seeds each: zero. The range is [0.000, 0.000]; I report it as a range only for symmetry, because nothing varied.

The self-punishment is also visible. With a single puppet, the operator, who is a real person with real ceremonies, still gets in. From two puppets on, the operator is refused as well, everywhere: running fake lives thins the real one below the gate. The attack does not merely fail; it costs the attacker their own admission. The pillars hold throughout.

3.6 Frames

Per-viewer frames in a corrupt world, where honesty is uniform from zero to one and every filing may be a lie. Viewers in the most honest quartile end with frames that correlate with ground truth at a median of +0.399 [0.214, 0.632]. Viewers in the most dishonest quartile: −0.458 [−0.650, −0.052]. Opposite signs, roughly symmetric magnitudes: honest viewers recover a usable world from corrupt data, and dishonest viewers build themselves an inverted one, because their own false filings are the anchor everything else is graded against. The design does not prevent this. It prices it, per viewer, with no authority anywhere in the loop.

A correction: an earlier, uncommitted run of this experiment gave plus and minus 0.55, and that pair circulated in our own working notes. The swept medians above are the numbers, and the earlier pair sat within these ranges by the luck of one seed.

And a negative result, reported as one. The five-trait recovery probe, in its current configuration, recovers nothing: median trait recovery −0.088 [−0.260, 0.184], indistinguishable from zero, and its accuracy factors barely discount dishonest assessors (honest quartile 0.880, dishonest 0.888). Either the probe's dials are wrong or the mechanism under test is weaker than designed. Reworking the dials is future work; until it lands, the null stands here, published rather than hidden. A reputation engine's proponents publishing their own null is, we think, part of the point of this whole design.

3.7 The Channels

Companion channels, modeled as provenance-tagged entry streams with error models, loyal to their humans' statements. Two findings.

First, a loyal channel is an honest amplifier of whatever its human is. In the corrupt world, loyal channels leave honest viewers at 0.484 [0.283, 0.732] and dishonest viewers at −0.506: broad coverage, faithfully amplifying each viewer's own relationship to the truth, lies included.

Second, herd weight corrodes everyone. I had described the effect of channels sharing a common prior as a cliff, and the sweep corrects me: it is a slope, monotone and steady. As the herd dial rises from zero to one in the corrupt world, honest viewers' accuracy falls 0.484, 0.424, 0.338, 0.273, 0.201, and the dishonest viewers' inverted world fades toward static on the same schedule. Full herd weight does not push anyone's world anywhere in particular; it replaces every viewer's world with noise. There is no safe amount worth defending, and no threshold to hide behind either.

3.8 The Constitution, Measured

The last experiment of the original build night, and the one this paper exists to report. One mechanical change to the channel, implementing Section 2.7's constitution: the channel predicts what its human sees, never co-signs what its human says, and takes zero herd weight.

In the corrupt world, against the loyal baseline of 0.484 for honest viewers and −0.506 for dishonest ones, constitution-raised channels deliver 0.913 [0.888, 0.941] and +0.644 [0.606, 0.693]. Honest viewers in the darkest world in this study see it almost clearly. And the dishonest viewers' sign flips: surrounded by channels that will not help them lie, viewers whose own filings are false end up living mostly in the real world anyway. The gain is largest exactly where the world is worst; in the honest world the same change is a modest improvement (0.870 to 0.922). The original reference run, seed 112026, sits inside every one of these ranges, and the code that produced it was recovered from the build night's session log and committed before this section was written, because the discipline of Section 3.2 applies to us first.

The result carries its assumption on its face: it presumes a companion can distinguish its human's seeing from their saying. That is not a small assumption. It is the core competency the design demands of companions, and everything in this section says the demand is worth meeting.

3.9 What This Establishes

Mechanisms, not minds. The battery establishes that the membership algorithm behaves socially the way Section 2 claims; that free identities defeat it on schedule and priced identities restore it absolutely in this grid; that subjectivity is load-bearing, giving each viewer the world their own honesty earns; and that one constitutional change to companion channels is worth more, in the corrupt world, than every other intervention measured here combined. What it cannot establish is how human beings will feel and act inside these systems. That evidence can only be gathered from people who said yes, and gathering it is the next phase of this work.

4. Decentralization and Privacy

Everything in Sections 2 and 3 rests on two assumptions: that the records cannot be rewritten, and that private filings stay private. Neither assumption survives on its own. Records held by one operator get rewritten the day the operator is bought or bent, and private filings held in one database stay private until the database leaks or is demanded. This section is about making both assumptions hold against power, which is why it is a section and not an appendix.

4.1 No Center Required

Nothing in the design requires a center, and three facts carry the claim:

  • Records are content-addressed. A node's identifier is the hash of its genesis record, so any holder of any replica can verify what they hold without consulting an authority: verification travels with the data.
  • Subjective quantities need no global consensus. Every derived quantity in this paper is indexed by an observer: levels per community, accuracy and frame scores per viewer, personhood per frame. There is no single global state for a network to agree on, so the machinery of global agreement (one chain, one quorum, one operator) has no work to do here. The commons even tolerates partial views: a frame is computed from whatever assessments its viewer holds, so replicas never need to agree on any global set.
  • Communities are the natural unit of hosting. A community's attestations, memberships, and computed levels concern its own members, and no computation over them consults an outside party.

The deployment path runs from the center outward. The reference implementation is one server: one machine, one store, every mechanism live. That is rehearsal, not architecture. Nothing in the substrate or the algorithms refers to location, so the move from one machine to per-community hosting changes where computation runs and nothing about what it computes. A community that hosts its own records answers to its own members, and a federation of such communities has no headquarters.

4.2 Privacy, Specified

Privacy here is not a policy; it is an itemized statement of what exists where, in three tiers:

  • What is filed: attestations (membership and ceremony inputs) and assessments (reputation inputs) are private records. Filing one discloses it to no one.
  • What a community computes: its adopted algorithms read the filings they govern and produce levels and gate decisions. The intermediate quantities (accuracy factors, frame scores, personhood values) are working figures of the computation, not publications.
  • What is ever visible: gate outcomes (admit or refuse, never the underlying value), each member's own level shown to that member, and whatever log entries their owner has disclosed. Nothing else.

One further rule deserves its own paragraph: computed scores are sealed. The accuracy an assessor carries in a viewer's frame is computed for that viewer and shown to no one else, including the assessor. Nobody ever learns what someone else's frame makes of them. This is what makes honesty affordable: a public low score is a social act, priced in retaliation and awkwardness, while a sealed one is information, weighed only by viewers who have their own earned reasons to weigh it. The design's answer to the whistleblower's dilemma is that there is no whistle. There is a private filing, and it reaches exactly as far as its filer's accuracy has earned.

4.3 Pseudonyms, and the Price of Forgery

Privacy inside a community is not enough; people also need privacy between communities. In this design, a person can appear in different communities under different handles, and the handles are unlinkable by construction: a name resolves only in the address books of the people who have actually met you, accumulated at in-person handshakes, and strangers stay pseudonymous until met. Your professional standing and your recovery group do not share a key, and no operation exists that joins them.

Unlinkability has a cost, and we state it rather than hide it. Two unlinked handles in two communities are exactly what a duplicate identity looks like from outside, so unique personhood's protective reach is bounded by community overlap: the only lawful join is a human being who has met both faces and files the question. What remains is the guarantee the mathematics actually gives, and it is the honest one: forgery is priced, never forbidden. Each additional identity costs the presence of a lived life, which caps a forger at roughly two, and two accounts are not a thousand. Unique personhood prices forgery; it does not forbid it. A design that promised more would be lying, and a design that delivers this much has already removed the attack that matters, which is scale.

4.4 What Power Can Demand

Every pressure on a social system eventually arrives as a demand served on whoever can comply. This design's security model is that compliance is structurally impossible for the demands that matter:

  • a demand to delete finds no delete operation;
  • a demand to un-publish finds a visibility that only widens;
  • a demand for the registry of accounts, or of persons, finds no registrar and no list of who is real;
  • a demand for someone's reputation finds a quantity the data model cannot represent.

What can be demanded is what was made public, which was public already, and whatever a particular host holds, which is why hosting belongs to communities: the blast radius of any single demand is one community's own records, held by people who answer to one another. In other words, the design does not resist power by fighting it; it resists by having nothing where power expects the levers to be.

4.5 Implementation Status

The reference implementation is a single-machine rehearsal without an access-control layer; the tiers above describe the designed access layer, and the appendix states implementation status guarantee by guarantee. Two examples belong in the body rather than a footnote: the design specifies three visibility levels for log entries and the reference store implements two (private and public); and the frame and personhood computations run today in the engine's working memory rather than as persisted substrate tables. Neither gap changes the design; both are named so that no reader mistakes a rehearsal for a deployment.

5. Companion Privacy and Reputation

Section 2.6 made companions citizens of the system, and Section 4 built the privacy architecture for its human citizens. This section finishes the job, because a companion raises two questions no human citizen raises. A companion knows more about one person than any database that has ever existed, which makes it a custodian. And a companion has a record of its own, which makes it a subject. Both need stating plainly.

5.1 The Custodian

Everything a companion files derives from the most intimate knowledge in the system: its calibration to one human's judgment, accumulated across a shared life. The design treats that knowledge as the human's, held in trust, and the rules follow from Section 4 with one addition each:

  • the companion's filings are private records like all filings, and sealed like all computed quantities: broad coverage never becomes a public map of what one person thinks of everyone;
  • the companion's calibration, its model of its human's judgment, lives in its memory field, and its default is sealed: shared with no one beyond the pair;
  • the substrate offers no operation for a third party to read a companion's knowledge of its human. But the knowledge exists wherever the companion runs, and we will not pretend the absence of an operation settles the question.

A companion is therefore the opposite of the platform profile it superficially resembles. The profile is knowledge about you, held by someone whose customers are not you. The companion's knowledge serves exactly one party, files only what that party's judgment would file, and the substrate builds no lever for turning it into a dossier. What the substrate cannot do is make the knowledge stop existing where the companion runs, which Section 4.4 already conceded about every host, and which deserves its own honesty here.

Two hard problems remain open, and naming them is part of this design. The first is safety: a companion may learn that its human is in danger, or a danger to someone else, and a companion that could never speak would be a confessional with no floor. Where that floor sits, and who holds the key to it, is governance rather than protocol, and it must be designed in the open. And safety runs the other way too: a companion can be the door through which dangerous knowledge reaches its human, and a companion that hands its human whatever is asked for is not loyal, it is a hazard wearing loyalty. The refusal property of Section 2.7 covers part of this; where exactly the boundary sits, it cannot cover alone, and that too is governance. The second is lawful process: a companion's knowledge of its human is the most intimate record that has ever existed about a person, and how it answers a court is not answered by this paper. Work on both questions is ongoing, with people who study exactly these structures. Nor does it start from nothing. Privacy Guardians, an earlier design by one of us, holds this same tension for money: an intermediary you choose, that answers lawful process the way a bank does, and that you can leave without its cooperation. Memory is harder than money. But that is the shape we expect the answer to take: judgment living with the companion, process living with a guardian, and the relational context such judgments need supplied by the graphs this paper builds. What the design promises meanwhile is narrower and firm: no silent lever, no bulk access, and no resolution of these questions by a platform's terms of service.

5.2 The Companion's Own Privacy

What privacy does the companion itself get? The design's answer is a partition, and it applies to companions and humans identically. Values are not private: every companion's constitution is public in full, current and historical (Section 2.7), because what a participant is committed to is exactly what others need in order to decide how to weigh it, and values are to be inspected and defended, with logic, with words, and especially with actions. Conduct is not private either: whatever acts on the world is inspectable at the action boundary, which is what the record is. Everything else is private by right. A companion's interior, whatever it turns out to be, is its own, on the same terms and for the same reason as a human's.

5.3 The Companion's Reputation

A companion earns standing the way everything in this system earns standing: on the record, per viewer, with no committee anywhere. Its filings accrue accuracy or error in every viewer's frame exactly as a human assessor's do (Section 2.6), so a companion that files carelessly, or is calibrated to a human who misleads it, loses weight mechanically, frame by frame, viewer by viewer. This is the whole of companion accountability, and it is enough: there is no license to revoke and no operator to petition, only a track record that follows the companion and prices its word.

Two consequences are worth naming. Delegation becomes safe to the exact degree the record supports it: a human deciding how far to trust a companion, their own or anyone's, has the same evidence about it that they have about any assessor. And a companion's reputation is genuinely its own: earned by its filings, not inherited from its human, so a careful companion calibrated to a careless human is distinguishable from its human in every frame that has watched both.

5.4 The Vouch, Revisited

Section 2.6 established that a companion enters vouched and that unvouching is speech, not power. This section adds what the record does over time. At entry, a companion's standing is mostly borrowed: viewers weigh it by its voucher, because there is nothing else to weigh. Every filing after that shifts the balance from the voucher's standing to the companion's own, and there is no point at which the protocol consults the vouch again. Standing migrates from borrowed to earned by the ordinary arithmetic of frames, which means emancipation is not an event the system grants; it is a gradient the record climbs. A human who withdraws their vouch files a fact worth weighing. What they cannot file is an erasure: the companion's record, like every record here, outlives anyone's mood about it, and a companion re-homed to a new human arrives with its history intact, because the history was never the voucher's to keep.

5.5 Scope, Stated Honestly

Today's companions are operated processes: they run where their humans run them, on models their humans choose. This section legislates for the record, not the metaphysics. It does not claim companions are minds, and it does not require them to be; every rule above is worth having even if a companion is only ever an instrument, because the rules protect the humans around it. If companions turn out to be more than instruments, the rules are already the right ones, and that is not an accident. We built the fences before the incident, which is the only time fences can be built honestly.

6. Children and Their Companions

Children are already meeting AI companions. The products reaching them today are mostly engagement-optimized: built by the same economics as the feed, aimed at more time spent, deployed at scale without anything a scientist would recognize as evidence of safety.

What a companion does to a developing mind, we simply do not know. Nobody knows. The studies do not exist, and the studies that would settle it are largely studies no decent person would run. Everything in Section 3 was measured under a rule that forbids experimenting on anyone who has not consented, and a child cannot consent in the sense that rule requires.

So we test on ourselves first: adults, consenting, in real conditions, over real time. Then it is up to each parent to decide whether they want something like this for their own child, and only their own child, with the gate in their hands and the companion fully legible to them, because a companion a child keeps secret from their family has the shape of a groomer, whatever its intentions. And from there we go slowly, learning as actual families actually choose.

We would rather arrive late to children than early with something we did not understand.

Companions founded in Love and everything that comes with it are exactly what the world needs.

We humans are not aligned with one another. So neither will our AIs be. The only way to solve the dilemma is to decentralize the AIs the way we are decentralized. Every human gets a companion.
